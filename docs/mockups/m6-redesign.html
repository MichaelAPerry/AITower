M6 redesign: research, advisors, lessons, graphics

The owner's playtest said the game hides its own ideas: research is a list, the primer floats, memos get ignored, and the tutorial does not teach. This document proposes one connected fix and shows every part as a mockup. Nothing here changes code yet. Mark up anything you want changed.

Summary

Every complaint has the same root: the game knows things the player cannot see or has no reason to care about. The fix is to make each idea visible, give it a voice, and tie it to a decision.

Research as a real tree

The tree below is built from the real tech.json: all 68 nodes, 8 lanes, 5 Levels. It shows a sample save at Level 2 so all four states are visible. Click a node for detail, click a lane name to isolate it, drag to pan, use the buttons to zoom.

Layout rules

Columns
Level 1 to 5, left to right. Columns above your Level are shaded and their nodes are dashed. You can see the future; you cannot buy it.
Lanes
One lane per branch: Administration, Logistics, Power, Cooling, Compute. Three path branches (Wetware, Green, Synthetic) sit below a rule and use the path colours. Green and Synthetic nodes currently sit inside the power and compute branches in the data, so we add a lane field.
Lines
Solid line = prerequisite within a lane. Dashed line = prerequisite from another lane (for example Cold-Plate Generation 3 needs Chilled-Water Loop Design). A line turns lane-coloured once its parent is researched.
States
Done (green tick), Available (cyan border), Locked (prerequisite missing, dim), Level-locked (dashed, shows L-number).
Detail
Cost in RP, what it unlocks (real equipment names), what it leads to, and a "Why go here" line from the relevant advisor.

Reasons to leave the first branch

  • Ceilings. Air stops scaling near 40 kW per cabinet. Cass says so once, when a rack passes 80% of its handler's capacity, and shows the Liquid branch as the way out.
  • Dashed lines. Cross-lane prerequisites tell the player "to go dense you need pipes and pumps" before they commit.
  • Capstones. Each lane ends in a Level 5 node that changes how the lane plays (Grid Autonomy Charter, Immersion Vault Study, Autonomous Maintenance Corps at Level 4).
  • Contracts. Clients ask for PF density or uptime that only certain branches reach. Lucien shows the offer and the branch that serves it.

Foreman and automation follow the ladder

  1. Line, not item. The Foreman gets a build spec per category: "Racks: Liquid line", "Cooling: match racks", "Power: match load". It builds the best item of that line you have unlocked, at your Level. Unlocking LC-3 makes the next Foreman rack an LC-3.
  2. Support first. Before a rack, the Foreman checks power headroom and cooling headroom. If short, the job queue shows "Air Handler AH-320, then rack". It never builds a rack that will overheat. (This also kills the false heat tips.)
  3. Tier cap. A dial per category: Standard, up to Advanced, up to Frontier. Default is the highest tier the player has paid for. Path items are never built automatically unless the path is chosen.
  4. Tell the player why. When the Foreman skips something, its status line says why: "Waiting: 160 kW needed, 110 kW free." Lucien or Bea offers the fix.
  5. Fleet Policy. The existing Fleet Policy Update tech (standing orders) holds the specs, so automation is a research reward and not a toggle.

04

Advisors

Names. The three specialists echo the MAGI, the three-part supercomputer in Neon Genesis Evangelion, itself named for the three wise men: Casper → Cass Parr, Balthasar → Bea Altasar, Melchior → Dr. Mel Kiorre. The CFO, Lucien Ferro, is a nod to Lucifer, the light-bringer, who brings light to the ledger and asks for something in return. Decisions that touch all three specialists can be shown MAGI-style: three votes, then Lucien names the price.

Four characters replace the UNIT-7 assistant and the memo desk. They are the only voices the game speaks in. Each owns a part of the datacenter, argues for it, and teaches the real concept behind it at the moment it matters.

Where they live on screen

One docked strip at the bottom of the world view, never over the build area. It shows at most one line at a time. A portrait chip on the right edge pulses once when a decision is waiting. The strip holds four portrait buttons: click one to ask that advisor "what should I worry about?", which gives a ranked answer from their area. This is the player-initiated path, so nothing has to nag.

Mockup: main HUD at 1440 wide with the Facilities advisor giving a heat tip. The tip has one concept, one Handbook link and one dismiss. Left tool bar and research tab shown in their current positions.

When and how they speak

Tips (non-blocking)

Triggered by a real condition in the sim, such as a handler above 85% load. One tip per concept per game, ever. Appears in the strip for 20 real seconds, then collapses to a portrait chip. Never stacks. At 30x speed tips queue, and the sim does not mention anything the player could not have seen.

Decisions (pause)

Replace memos. At most one per in-game day, never more than one per 3 real minutes at any speed. The game pauses when one opens. No timeout and no reputation penalty for time. The player may press "Defer one day" once per decision, and the advisor's recommended option is marked.

Asked (on demand)

Click a portrait, or the "Ask" button on a research node, build card or alert. The advisor answers from live numbers and links the Handbook. This is where "why wetware over racks" is answered in full.

Rule against the old failure mode. Reputation only changes from player choices and from actual outcomes (an SLA missed, an audit passed). It never changes because a prompt expired or the player played fast.

How they teach and how they argue

  • One concept per moment. Heat tip teaches "all electrical power ends up as heat". It does not also teach PUE.
  • Show the number, then the word. "The handler is at 92% of 150 kW" first; "capacity" goes into the Handbook link.
  • Argue with evidence. When a branch is available and affordable, its advisor makes the case once, with a comparison: "On your floor plan LC-3 gives 230 PF in the slot AC-1 gives 20."
  • Disagree with each other. Mel wants more output; Bea says the feeder cannot carry it; Lucien wants the bill. When one decision touches two areas the card shows both, one line each, then one recommendation. That is the interesting trade-off.
  • Link, do not lecture. Each concept ends with a Handbook chip that opens that article. The advisor text stays under 40 words.

Lesson screen

Mockup: Lesson 2, "Where the Heat Goes". The goal bar is part of the HUD, not an overlay. The Handbook chip opens "Heat and cooling" at the right place.

How Lesson 1 replaces the tutorial

  1. The title screen offers "Start Lesson 1" and "Free Build". New players land on Lesson 1 by default. No separate tutorial mode exists.
  2. Bea opens with the goal, not a tour. "Two racks, one day, no blackouts." The first rack is a single highlighted slot. Nothing else is selectable until it is placed, so there is nothing to get lost in.
  3. Learn by cause and effect. Placing the second rack trips the feeder (the "mistake" is designed). Bea explains kW versus capacity, the power overlay lights up, and the player adds the genset.
  4. A result screen. Stars for headroom and uptime, what you learned in one line, and the next lesson's title. Lessons are replayable and sit in the Lessons tab.
  5. Unlocks. Finishing a lesson earns a small starting-cash bonus for Free Build, so the campaign is worth doing but never required.

06

Visible maintenance and logistics

The repair loop is the most important thing in the game that the player cannot see. Failure creates a job, a job needs a bot, a part and a route. Each of those should be readable in the world and in one HUD view. This is design only; the bug fixes for bots, lifts and visibility are separate.

Mockup: world with an Ops view open. Numbered markers match the notes below.

  1. Fault marker. A failing rack gets a wrench badge, a red outline and a countdown ring. Hover shows cause and expected repair time (from bots.json: Maintenance Bot repair 30 ticks, Lifter 24).
  2. Bot state. Bots show a state icon above them: idle, moving, repairing, charging, waiting for part, waiting for lift. A dotted line draws the route to the current job. Selecting a bot shows battery (600 ticks for the Maintenance Bot) and its queue.
  3. Depot stock. The Spare Parts Depot shows stacked crates in the world matching its stock, and a gauge in the HUD: "Parts 7 / 12, 2 in transit". Low stock turns amber, empty red, and the Facilities advisor mentions it once.
  4. Lift routes. Each lift shows its queue as small pips, its direction arrows, and a dwell timer. Routes between floors are drawn as dotted vertical lines when a bot is travelling (lift.json: capacity 2, dwell 4 ticks).
  5. Repair queue. A list in the Ops tab: rack, fault, assigned bot, part status, ETA, cost of downtime per hour. The top job is the one losing the most money, and you can drag to reprioritise.
  6. Overlay toggle. One button (M) fades the world and highlights only maintenance objects, like the Heat and Power overlays in Oxygen Not Included.
07

Graphics update

The graphics must carry meaning the HUD can no longer carry alone: tier, state (cool, hot, failing, idle), and path. Three directions, each shown on the same scene: one air rack (Standard), one liquid rack (Advanced), one Frontier tank, a handler, a floor and a bot.

A. Clean cutaway

Flat fills with a consistent 1.5 px outline and one light direction. Tier shown as a coloured top plate. Calm and readable at any zoom. Lowest cost to produce.

B. Pixel art with lighting

Stepped pixel shapes, dithered shading, glowing LEDs and heat shimmer. Most atmosphere, most art labour, and the hardest to keep readable when zoomed out.

C. Bold flat vector

Big shapes, no outlines, saturated tier colours. Strong silhouettes and very readable, but loses the synthetic, technical mood of the HUD.

Recommendation

A with B's lighting. Use the clean cutaway for shapes, and add a lighting pass: status LEDs, a cyan under-glow for Standard and Advanced, amber for Frontier, and a heat tint that warms a rack as it nears its limit. This keeps the world readable, matches the glass-and-graphite HUD (STYLE_SYNTHETIC.md), and uses the engine's existing layers. It is the cheapest option that still looks like a real change.
